On this side, I on that, men do their fortunes
Drop off, like leaves in autumn; yet turn not out
Into fantastical schemes, which the long lives
In the world's kale of indigeneous days
Could scarce have leisure for, fools that we are!—
Never to think of Death & of ourselves
At the same time; as if to learn to live
Were no concern of me. O more than I think!
For creatures of a day, in genetion more
To fettle in Eternity's dead hand
Unapprehensive; when for night we leave
The very first mortal surge shall sweep us in!—
'Tis but a night, a long & moonless night,
We make the peace on bed, & then are gone.
Thus at the shut of even, the weary bird
Leaves the wide air, & some lonely trunk
Encloses down, & I dozes till the dawn of day
Then claps his well fledged Wings & leaves among!
(147)
Let cheerful memory from her forest cell
Send forth a gentle train of Pastoral fair
Cherished in earliest youth, now paying back
With lenihood thorns the fruits bare;
And pouring in my Wounds the heavenly balm
Of conscious Innocence.— But chiefly, Then,
When left in my Rely once laid down from Heaven,
To this for Man, to teach him how to live,
And oh! still harder lesson! how to die,
Dividing not then to smooth the pathless bed
Of tributes & of pain, to give the then
That fertile Nature drops, calm all her fears
With all her hopes, & animate her faith
Till my unprayed soul, anticipating Heaven
Bursts from the Thraldom of unremembering gloom
And in the wing of Eternity's light & Life—
Dearly by D. Pertinax
Death is only to be felt, never to be suffered
Even by those that touch it!
H. W. Spalding
