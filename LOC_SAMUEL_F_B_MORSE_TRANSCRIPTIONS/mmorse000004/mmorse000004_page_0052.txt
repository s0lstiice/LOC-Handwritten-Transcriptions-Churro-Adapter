My friend and benefactor.
Your letter of the 28th February I received enclosing
two pounds, and should have answered it before, but considering the
nebulous situation, as I had nothing particular to communicate.
I have been in a great degree of distress, and my situation has been preserved
through a long season of miserable captives. I have not every occasion in my power to do anything for myself, and thus far
have made my situation more comfortable than could have been expected, with the means. I have waited with patience the
will of an inevitable providence, by which all things are overtaken for the best possible good, and have been flattered with the hope
that the late events in Europe would effect a universal peace, and that my liberty was not far distant. At present I cannot form no opinion as to the result of the negotiations, but hope to hear your opinion, and that you will have the goodness to inform me if any thing appears favorable. I find my health
and strength gradually dying, and in consequence of it, have laboured under much affliction for the month past. The French prisoners are now about to return to their respective homes, and the prison is in a state of revolution. We are soon to be removed from this to another prison, it is said on board the prison ships at Baltimore. I hope I shall not suffer so much on the journey as before -- If it is in your power, I hope to receive a little more assistance, as my situation is now much altered by ill health, and, the departure of the French prisoners. I am much in want of your kind attention, as I have not received any letters from America, -- the Point informed me the other day that he had not letters by the Ship Fair America by which any friends would get information concerning me --
