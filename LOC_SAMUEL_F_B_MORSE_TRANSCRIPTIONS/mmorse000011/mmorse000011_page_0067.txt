In May, 1834, I returned to New York. I spent
the summer partly at the residence of Mr. Zell, son
a nephew of Edward Loring, on the banks of the Hudson,
in aideremain excursion to the coal mines of Penna.
at New Port R.I., etc. in New York city. In New York city,
I frequently visited Prof. More, and was with him on
one occasion when the advent of George Thompson, M.P.,
and the beginning of the abolition excitement in Massachusetts,
revived my recollection of what had been told me in
warning, some months since. About the same time
a Dr Cox, living at Elecker Street, I think, had married
a negro to a white woman, and the indignation caused
had gutted his home in revenge. I spoke to Cox, and
he repudiated the idea, especially of John Quincy Adams
having any thing to do with it, and invited me to attend
an abolition meeting that night. The miserable, derisive,
flying speech made on the occasion, disgusted all
moderate men, and my friend asked "are the northern
people such fools as to listen to such as that?" My
reply was, "if I have read history, eight out of ten
of the people are always right at first, but when
passion is aroused, they never reason." He then
heard, what I shall never forget "The Northern people
will never sacrifice Constitution & laws under the
lead of fools of fanaticism to stay the goad that lays the
golden egg." I resolved already, because I began to
