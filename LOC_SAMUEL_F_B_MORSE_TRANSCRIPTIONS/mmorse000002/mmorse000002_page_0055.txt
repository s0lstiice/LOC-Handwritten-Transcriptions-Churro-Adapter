usually composed of three parts viz. two wings and a center
now to have these constructed according to the strict rules
of uniformity, the wings should be perfectly similar to each
other, should have a due proportion to the center and
the entrance should be in the center at equal distances
from the sides, this constitutes uniformity,
but if the door instead of being placed in the middle
should be thrust in at one side the eye would directly
perceive the defect and indicate to the mind
that something was wrong. Thus much for the
principles of beauty considered abstractly, it now only
remains to find a character most suitable to exhibit
all these principles combined; and when shall we
find a better example than man? The dissimilarity
of the eyes, nose, ears, and mouth to each other are exhibits
of variety; the elegant construction of the limbs so well
calculated to perform their respective offices as fair examples
of proportion, & the similarity of one eye, ear, arm, &c. to
its fellow are excellent specimens of Uniformity; So that from
what has been said we may draw this conclusion, that
real Beauty cannot exist without Variety, Proportion,
and Uniformity; and that man is consequently the
best standard of Beauty, as he possesses all these qualities
in a high degree of perfection.
February 21st 1809.
Samuel F. B. Morse
