My dear Sir
Baltimore Oct 7th 1844,
In addition to what I have written respecting the Telegraph Office here, I thought it proper that I should again intimate with the observation and remarks now being made respecting the Games of Chess, at the Telegraph Office here. As perhaps without explanation the fact may seem to prejudice the public mind, I own this is somewhat difficult especially when they do not listen to explanation.
I have been enquired of several times if such was the fact, I replied I believe something of the kind was done & merely to test the capacity of the Telegraph -- but it doesn't seem to satisfy their curiosity so long as it exists, especially when strangers seize the Office to use, as in the case may be, to find they may not be admitted to witness its function for the reason of it being thus occupied, or even if admitted then, an impression unfavorable is carried with them.
It is the susceptibility of the people, perhaps more one now so, I know of nothing to the contrary but others equally so, I stand in this kind of sentence (of that the word) and would like to participate, but find them seldom included by previous complaint; And thus in fact may be the only to cause the doubt does not rest solely by the ear, and the most thing the feared, is some foolish spirit in the papers proposing to the contrary. The Roger Button with whom I made you acquainted when you were here, I by the way, a valuable young man a member of one of the Chambers here, spoke to his brother Henry, as to what he had been enquired of H.R. -- I think the anxiety of Mr. Roger as he enquired of me, if I had heard much said of this? -- No man could be better qualified to fill the Station he occupies, always prompt, vigilant, attentive to the true interests of the Telegraph.
