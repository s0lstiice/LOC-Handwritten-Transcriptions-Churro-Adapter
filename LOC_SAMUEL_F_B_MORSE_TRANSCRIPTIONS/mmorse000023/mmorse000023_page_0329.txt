To the business men of Poultrney, Granville, Salem, Button Ville, Union Village, Schaghticoke Point, Lansingburgh, and all others who may be interested in the construction of a Line of Telegraph, between Troy and Whitehall, with an office in each of the villages named above, including Troy and Whitehall.
Expense of Operator, office, rent, fuel, &c., I put at $200 for each office, that being the cost already paid in villages similarly situated to those above named, where Telegraphs have already been established, and that sum will also pay for doing the business of this Line, in either the Troy or Whitehall office, and for five office years, $200 each.

The above estimate is amply sufficient to defray the expenses of working this contemplated Line of Telegraph, even if it should do a larger amount of business than I shall estimate for it; and as business increases so as to occupy more of the operators' time to attend to it than I have estimated, their salaries can also be increased in a corresponding ratio.
The business of attending a Telegraph Register is very fascinating, and there will be more difficulty in making choice of the proper person from the number of applicants that will offer their services, than there will be to find men to take the offices, at the prices I have named. The Telegraph Register must be placed in a Store, Lawyer's Office, Post Office, a Jeweler or Harness Maker's Shop and even many other places suitable persons can be found to take charge of it, as it would not occupy necessarily more than three feet square of room. Females may also be employed to attend a Register if they possess the requisite skill.

The above table shows daily receipts at the 7 offices, amounting to $18.10, or $5,647.20 for a year of 32 days. From which deduct the expenses, and 7 per cent. on the capital for one year, which is $3,625, and we have a surplus of $2,022.20, or about 14 per cent. on the capital, making in all, 21 per cent. profit on the business of the Line. Now this may appear like a large estimate, to those persons who have given but little attention to the subject of this new and extraordinary mode of conveying intelligence. But my experience, based upon the operations of other similar lines of Telegraph, justify me in expressing the opinion, that the estimate will be fully realized, the moment the Line is put in operation.
Many persons entertain the opinion that the business for a Telegraph is confined to a few individuals, who are styled as business men; this is an error. All classes of society contribute more or less business to the Telegraph. Merchants use it to order goods, make inquiries respecting the markets and negotiate sales of produce. The Lawyer uses the Telegraph to keep himself informed of the progress of business in Court, that he may know when his own cause is likely to be reached, without the expense and loss of time incident to waiting in person on the slow progress of justice. The Banker has frequent and important uses for the Telegraph. With its aid he can so regulate his affairs, as to preserve for his customers a sound and uniform currency.
The farmer uses the Telegraph to inquire the price in market of such articles of produce as he has to sell whenever he suspects the purchaser of offering too low a price, and while he is feeding his team, he is also preparing himself to meet his customer on equal footing. The Hotel Keeper uses the Telegraph in giving and receiving information respecting the movement of travelers. Physicians have occasional use for the Telegraph in consulting distant Physicians, or inviting them to attend some obstinate case as counsel. Stage proprietors find the Telegraph very convenient to regulate their business in ordering horses, inquiring after their teams during bad state of the roads, giving directions on the brack down occurs, &c., &c. Travelers have very frequent use for the Telegraph, and always give preference to routes having such accommodations, other things being equal. They keep their friends at home advised of the progress of their journey, the state of their health, giving directions and making requests about their business at home. &c., &c. Persons about leaving home on business will first inquire if the persons are at home with whom they have business and inform them of the time of their visit so as to ensure a meeting. Parties of pleasure visiting neighboring villages use the Telegraph to announce their intended visit, and order such accommodations as the nature of the case may demand. And all persons use the Telegraph more or less for social purposes—inquiring after the health of distant friends, giving information of the state of their own health, informing of deaths, marriages, births, &c. Communications of all these different characters I have seen presented at the office of Telegraph Stations.
The prices I have named in the estimated receipts are, in some instances, lower than is charged on any Line of Telegraph now in operation; in others, the same. As Telegraphs become better known they will be more used, and the prices charged will be reduced.
With these remarks I will submit the question to your consideration, whether the interests of your several villages, would not be advanced by erecting a Line of Telegraph from Troy to Whitehall, by which they would be connected at two points, with the great American Telegraph system.
E. CORNELL.
Whitehall Jan. 21, 1849.
