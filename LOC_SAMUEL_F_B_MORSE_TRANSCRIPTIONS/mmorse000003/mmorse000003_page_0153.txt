Bloomingdale, New York March 4th 1843.
My dear friend:
I am fearful you will hardly remember, now as one when you
invited to enter the list of your correspondents, and I am well aware that almost
two years inattention to your request does not entitle me to such remembrance.
I intended however that you should receive the letters of my husband
as from me, inasmuch as he is now by proxy, and I confess I am quite offended at you for not
having addressed me line to one in return. -- We were much gratified by
your letter which gives us so pleasant an acquaintance as your friend Mr King; but
we should have been better pleased if he had delivered it earlier that we might have
had more of his company. -- I believe he has established himself at Philadelphia
which is indeed a more eligible situation for an artist than this city affords, and
where, I doubt not, he will receive very flattering encouragement. -- I am in general
ab proved of the spirit of my country men; but there is too little attention paid to the
fine arts, to merit taste and science. Man here is weighed by his purse not by
his mind, and according to the preponderance of that, he rises or sinks in the
scale of individual opinion. A fine pointing or marble statue is very rare in the
houses of the rich of this city, and those individuals who would not pay fifty pounds
for either, expect no doubt that sum to go with a napkin in a piece of furniture.
But do not tell tales. I would not say this to an Englishman, and I trust
you have not yet become one. This however is poor encouragement for you to return
to your native country -- I hope better things of that country before you may return;
and I sincerely believe that the time is not far distant when a West (of such another
star should use no other horizon) and a Vanderbilt may be restored amongst us --
He is left
