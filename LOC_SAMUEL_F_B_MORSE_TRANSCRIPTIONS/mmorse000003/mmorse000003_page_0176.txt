and though I shall never regret this unhappy war, yet I
shall ever reflect with pride upon the occasion which, has
brought before the world a Hull, a Decatur, a Jones, a
Bainbridge, and a Lawrence; — Hull was the first who
struck the flag of English invincibility, and nailed it
to the American mast. Hull was the first who struggled
with the English seamen, and I wonder what
might have been expected when our naval heroes attained
maturity, and Hull was the first to teach the
Americans themselves where to look for future safety
and future power. To Hull then is due the first and
greatest blessing Americans can bestow, and America's
grateful homage he has received, he has obtained from sister
to the English, thus is due the first lesson America can
bestow — not to trust on
guilty men, but on God and His counsels, of which you will find sufficient
proof in your letter over many times
with great interest, and every time with increased interest.
Will reading it give you cause of sorrow, and if
recently read the important scenes of the New Haven siege
you will understand my feelings in your company; the
political parties at the Talbot's where I monopolized
you as the partner, the tea party where I put my hand
in my pocket and other books to you, an
eye for writing you, and a knife for me on the
back my heart was with me, our country's watch, when
I think myself the happiest mortal breathing, the liberty
of the United States and the Constitution
of the United States are the two things which I
have to be happy in, and I am happy in them.
There are pleasures too exquisite to be described, after
which I almost forget myself on earth.
