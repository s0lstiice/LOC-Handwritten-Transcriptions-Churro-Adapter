the ruling party in America, were very much under French
influence; I replied No, that I believed, on the contrary
that nine tenths of the American people were more disposed
strongly in favor of this Country, as a proof I urgeded, the
universal prevalence of English fashions in preference to
French, English manners & customs; the resounding on the
succès of the English over the French, the marked attention
shown to English travellers & visitors, the neglect with
which they treated their own literary productions, for an account
of the strong prejudice in favor of English works;
that every thing in short was enhanced in its value by
having attached to it the name English. — On the other hand
I told them that the French were a people almost universally
despised in America, and by at least one half hated; as in
England they were esteemed the common enemies of mankind,
that French fashions were discontenanceed & loathed, that
a Frenchman was considered as a man always to be suspected,
that young men were forbidden, in many instances
to associate with them, considering their company & habits
as tending to subvert their morals & to render them frivolous
& insincere. I added, that in America as well as every
where else there were bad men, men of no principles,
whose consciences never stand in the way of their ambition or
avarice, but that I firmly believed that as a body the American
Congress was as free from corruption & foreign influence
