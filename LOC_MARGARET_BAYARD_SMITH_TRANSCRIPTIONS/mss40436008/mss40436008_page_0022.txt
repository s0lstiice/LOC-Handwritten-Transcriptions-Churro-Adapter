Observations on Poetry.
There may be a certain self-surrender of propriety, given on
relations of what is now called taste (an idol that
has misruined our styles;) but Dante and Ariosto
were ambitious of conveying to the minds of others
the impressions on their own, with force and persua-
siveness, and to affect this they could not
refuse to stoop to the meanest images.
Indeed, in spite of all his romance, Ariosto is the poet of
Nature; his images, and he abounds in them beyond almost
any other writer, are from things which seem all to
have come under his own observation. They have all the
simplicity and spirit of proof impressions. They are not
refined from ancient and improved artists, which by
transformation through various hands have lost
the most delicate features of the model,
but they are original drawings by a most original
master. Hence the mighty mixture of the new
and magnificent which they sometimes present,
because it is only by exhibiting natural objects
in unnatural proportions that this can be kept
out of sight. Hence, again, the distinctness of their
images, which when they are introduced for the
purpose of occasion to make use of a lion or a
bear (and what poet has not?) he exhibits them
them in a circus or a cage, or with a rascal about
their necks, more frequently than amidst their
natural haunts; because in the one situation
he had probably seen them with his own eyes,
in the other he must have taken his information
on trust, if he would describe a stream of blood
thinning the armour of a wounded warrior, he compares it to the purple ribbon
which he had one day received from his master, nothing
upon ground of setting a white (exceeds of a Diana in
a by there will serve him a good turn, his were
apt to refer his readers for them to the Hercules
theatre than to Selos or the Sophian theatre.)
