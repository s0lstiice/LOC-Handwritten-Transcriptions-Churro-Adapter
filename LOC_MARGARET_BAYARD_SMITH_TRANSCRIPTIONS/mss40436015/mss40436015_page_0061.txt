"I must set about some employment soon, my heart begins to eat itself again."
"I do not know that I am happier alone, but this I am sure of, that I am never long in society without over-earning for my lamps & my utterly confused & tumble'd over library."
"I ride hard for an hour daily, to attenuate & dissipate the physical part of me -- The more violent the fatigue, the better my spirits for the rest of the day, & then my evenings have that calm nothingness of languor which I most delight in."
"I went out very little last year & mean to go about still less. I have no passion for circles & have long regretted that I ever gave in to what is called a town life, which of all the lives I ever knew seems to me to leave the least for the past or the future."
It is indifference which makes me so uncouth & apparently effeminate -- It is not elegance for new parents, but that nothing interests me sufficiently to fix. Neither do I feel disgusted, but simply indifferent. The proof of this is, that obviously, the slightest error, slight men, in most cases opposition is a stimulus, in mine it is not -- if a scheme is in my way I could not stop it from passing it off."
(Indifference! it is the poison of the soul, the most unengagingable -- irreparable fault of all mental diseases)
The truth is, I fear, that rarely, if ever have men of the higher orders of genius allowed themselves futter for the calm affections & comforts that form the cement of domestic life. One misfortune, says Pope, of extraordinary geniuses is, that their very friends are more apt to admire them than love them -- Such must generally be the lot of all preeminently gifted minds -- the very qualities that enable them to command and admiration are those that too often incapacitate them from cultivating love -- the habits of abstraction & self-interest to which the occupation of men of genius leads, are in themselves of an unusual & debasing tendency --
Moore
