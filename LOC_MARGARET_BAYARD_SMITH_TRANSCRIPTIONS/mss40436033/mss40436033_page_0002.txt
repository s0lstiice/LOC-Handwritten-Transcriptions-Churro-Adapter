July 6. 1829. Sidney.
My dear Sister, I am not well, but as I do not know
when I shall be better, I will ever write. The only letter
may be a rude one, it will at least give my remains
known to my affectionate sympathy in your joy to
arrive at Bayard I hear he is arrived, after a prosperous
& advantageous voyage -- or this must give me a
debt of fully reward from the anxiety I felt when
you wrote -- for that I give you joy & hope every other
care may be removed from your mind. With -- by
this time is well, & all this year, I hear one confidant
into your article, I hear your mind still suffer
less, than it did while Dear Brother was ill. You
have exact what remedies are necessary, & that
a painful complaint, occasioning an interference
with to your room, it brings with it some of that
alarm which many other diseases do. I think I have
heard you say, you enjoyed the quiet & stillness consequent
from this hour of imprisonment, as I did
not prevent your occupying either hands or mind --
Is the complaint as painful now as it used to be?
or is it only weakness that is exerted? Whatever
be of you being in your own room, I wish to be
vict of you more than at any other time. -- How especially
I shall enjoy myself very much in aiding you in fancy
works for your hair, from which I have say you immense
amusement -- you must write me a very particular account
of it. The 15th of July will be quite a great day for you
in Brown's a commencement in fair, and the town
full of people! -- I should indeed like to be one of the
company, the picture it would be rather too exciting for
my most excitable frame, in whichettle things, either
pleasantable or painful, anxious, thoughtful or
desperately troublesome, or indifferent.
