If I should not therefore be mistaken in this my firm persuasion, you will have reason, my son, when death
shall have removed me from your power to remove me
as a cruel and selfish spirit. But although the will
should perish with the body, I cannot rend it to you,
nevertheless, to honor me my memory with a pure and
invincible regard in the divine father and immortal judge by
whose power and providence this beautiful fabric of the
universe is sustained and governed. Such were the
sentiments of the dying Cyrus, permit me now to express my own. Dear Hope, consider that your illustrious
ancestors, together with many other excellent persons whom I dare not list particularly name, would
have so evidently undertaken to erect the lion and the
lioness barrier of Fortitude, had they not been persuaded
that they had a real interest in the opinion which
future generations might entertain concerning them.
And, so you imagine, my noble friends (if I may be
adjudged in an old man's parlance to know them all),
do you imagine I would have undergone these labours
I have sustained both in my civil and military engagements,
if that supposed that the conscientious submission I received from the glory of my actions, would terminate with my present existence? If such had
been my punishment would it not have been far
better and more rational, to have passed my days in
an unhurried and indolent repose without labour
and without contention? But my mind, by I know
not what secret impulsion, even in my old age, strongly persuaded that I should then
only begin to live, when I ceased to exist in the present world.
