be to the want about selfish population, and the author-
itation of a period when the natural ambition of wealth gives
place to the love of distinction and power. It strikes us that
the idleness, the vice and the misery of what is termed
a surplus population, regrettably, in a great degree, in those
three broad inequalities of wealth, which are generated
and rendered perpetual by the maxims and institutions
of patriarchal and aristocratical governments. We do
not believe, that in the general distribution of the blessings
of Providence, and where things are suffered to take
their natural course, there will ever be such a surplus
population in any country, as will necessarily entail
upon the large mass of the people, either ignorance,
idleness, profligacy, or want. We cannot for a moment
bring our mind to a repetition of the modern doctrine
of surplus population, which indiscriminates the picture as
well as the energy of Heaven, in the supposition that
it licenses, as it does, a party to the existence of millions
of human beings, predestined to watchfulness, and as a
matter of necessity subjected to all the penalties of vice
and poverty combined. We believe most sincerely that
such monstrous evils are almost entirely the fruits of a
bad government; the product of that cunning expedient
which gives all to the few, and nothing to the
many; which diverts the providences of God from their
natural and salutary course; which widens from the
mass of mankind their best support, in the product
of their own labours, to exchange it for a sole dependence
upon alms. Where such a system prevails, as it
does prevail on a most extraordinary large portion of
the world, the predominance of idleness and beggary
is not owing to the excess of population. It arises
either from a helplessness of bringing their condition
by any exertions, or from the certainty that charity will
at last partially supply, what of itself never has been
away. It is, we think, idle to assert, that either Italy,
