Paybrook November 1810.
Upon my return from New York a few days since I received from my
Mother the package of books for myself and the one for Sister, which you were so kind
as to ask me to accept of. I stayed but a few hours in New Haven and had not an
opportunity to read the letter which accompanied it, until I was seated in the carriage
which conveyed me to I Brook. Iannel will herself thank you for the compliment
to her -- it is a pretty edition of Scott's works -- and for that to me I beg you to accept
my grateful acknowledgments. I will not promise to think of you when I read Scott,
but if you will be content to have me think of you when I see his works, I will promise as
much. -- You ask me, my amiable friend, to request Iannel to give you an equal
claim to your affection, with that of any other person, or to esteem you as one indifferent.
She is discreet, she is generous, and the fundship she entertains for you will, I presume,
secure to you what you request me to obtain. -- I have ever considered that
young lady unfortunate whose affections are won upon her first entrance into life, before
her judgment is matured and while her experience and knowledge of mankind are too
circumscribed to allow her to distinguish between real and fictitious merit. -- If her choice
should prove a proper one she is too young to enter into that state, which requires much
prudence and knowledge to make those who enter it happy and respectable; -- if she does
enter it and by any misfortune sees her prospects blighted and her situation less happy
than she has expected, she reflects upon herself, or what is more cruel, upon her husband
