of the reaction with which my Telegraph shall meet from
the Emperor. M Angot speaks of improvements he has made in
the Telegraph, although he does not describe their nature. I presume they relate to his ingenious language, and the adaptation of it to rapid correspondence. I have so changed the form of the apparatus, and condensed it into so small a compass that
you would scarcely know it for the same instrument which you saw in Paris. I have added to it a piece of mechanism which not merely puts the register in movement to write, but stops it when the message is written, and without the presence of any attendant So that a communication is not lost from any accidental absence of the attendant.
The General Melnikoff whom you command
