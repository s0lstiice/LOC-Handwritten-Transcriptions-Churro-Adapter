On - Happiness
The mind during the greater part of
its existence is employed in active
engagements, exertions, not in merely
attending to its own feelings of pain &
pleasure; 2 the list of its faculties,
understanding, memory, foresight, sentiment,
wish & intention, only contains the
names of its different operations.
If in the absence of every sensation
to which we commonly give
the names of enjoyment or suffering,
our very existence may have its
offensive qualities of happiness or
misery; and if what we call pain
& pleasure, occupies but a small
part of human life, compared to
what passes in contrivance execution
in pursuits & spectacles, in conduct,
reflection & social engagements,
it must appear that our active
purseacts, at least on account of
their duration, deserve the greater
part of our attention. When their
occasions have failed the
demand is not for pleasure,
but for something to do, & the very complaints
of the sufferer is not so sure
a mark of his distress, as the state
of the languid.
We seldom however, reckon any taste
which we are bound to perform,
among the blessings of life. We always
aim at a period of pure enjoyment,
or a termination of trouble,
& overlook the source from which most
of our present satisfactions are probably
drawn: ask the busy, where is the
happiness to which they aspire? They
will answer perhaps that it is found
in the object of some present pursuit.
If we ask why they are not
miserable in the absence of that object,
they will say that they hope to attain
it. But is it hope alone that sustains
the mind in the midst of precarious
& uncertain prospects? And would
sparance of success fill the intervals
of expectation with more pleasing emotions?
