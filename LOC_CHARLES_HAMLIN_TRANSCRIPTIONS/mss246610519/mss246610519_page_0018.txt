That was in May 1908. It was not until four years later,
namely, in January 1912, that the Aldrich Committee transmitted to
Congress the report of the National Monetary Commission.
Whether the report of that commission materially influenced
the currency bill that subsequently became known as the Federal Re-
serve Act, is still a matter of controversy and of widely divergent
opinions. The paternity of the Federal Reserve Act seems to have
been as multifarious as that of some of the war babies in France and
Belgiumium. Nevertheless there can be no question that, as between the
Aldrich-Warburg conception of a banker-controlled currency system or
banking system, and the Wilson (not Glass) conception of a government-
controlled system, the views of the National Monetary Commission did
not prevail in 1913.
In other words, the action of Congress in appointing a
commission in 1908, instead of passing a law in 1908, resulted in
the bankers getting in 1913 what they then certainly regarded as radical
legislation. Furthermore, the organized opponents of the Currency Bill
of 1913 made in that year a vigorous plea for postponement of action
pending further study of the proposed legislation. In this strenuous
effort to defer action they had the full support of the big metropolitan
newspapers and the financial press.
