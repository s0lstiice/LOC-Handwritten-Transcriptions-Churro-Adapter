Tuesday, April 15, 1920:
Governor Harrison appeared before the Board to give
an account of his recent visit to Europe. He said that
central bankers abroad felt that there was no great shortage
of capital in the world, but the trouble was its bad
distribution; that there had been great deflation abroad
and a collapse of prices; that they believed that no rates
for short-term borrowings would help long-term borrowings,
bonds, etc., by driving short-term into long-term credits;
that they all recognised the danger that easy money may
incur in speculation, not only in the United States but in
Europe, that basic commodities have fallen in price all
over the world; that purchasing power has been diminished;
that a general denial was made that banking policy abroad
had caused deflation, and any obligation to stabilize prices
was also denied.
He said that there was great curiosity about the
operations of the Federal Farm Board, and how much grain
they would buy; that the situation was a world situation
which the Farm Board could not hope to control; that Russia
was selling raw materials abroad far below cost in order to
get capital, e.g., that lumber was sold at prices which
Sweden could not meet; that there was really no cost of
production in Russia in our sense of the term; that Russia
was even shipping coal to Boston at less than our domestic
prices; that if Russia should dump wheat abroad, the
situation would become intolerable; that they saw no remedy
for this situation except that America was giving credit
to Russia on extremely low terms, and that our credit
should be embargoed; that Americans usually got 50% cash,
which protected them, and all else in the shape of notes
represented profits; that Americans should cut down Russian
credits; that gold and long-term credit policy was the
vital question; that the United States and some foreign
countries could stand the loss of gold provided it was
shipped to places where it was needed.
