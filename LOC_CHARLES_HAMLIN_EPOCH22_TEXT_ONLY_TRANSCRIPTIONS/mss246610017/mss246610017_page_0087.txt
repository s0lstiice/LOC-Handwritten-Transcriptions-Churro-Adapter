Since the dissolution of the Capital Issues Committee on
August 30, 1919, the markets of the United States have been open,
at least so far as the Federal Reserve System is concerned, to
offerings of all security issues, whether domestic or foreign.
The fact that the statutes governing one European central
bank have provided that no offerings shall be made by it in
the United States except after approval by one of the Federal
reserve banks, -- the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, -- which
action may speedily be followed by similar statutes governing
other foreign central banks, -- does not, in the opinion of the
Federal Reserve Board, give either to the Board or to any Federal
reserve bank any such authority.
The approval or disapproval either by the Board or by any
Federal reserve bank, of any such foreign security offerings,
might, in the opinion of the Board, entail serious consequences
to the future credit situation, and might hamper the Federal
Reserve System in its future credit policies.
The Federal Reserve Bank of New York should, therefore,
be advised to notify the Bank of International Settlements in
reply to its communication, and as well, the other principal
central banks, that approval by the Board or by any Federal
reserve bank is not necessary as a condition precedent to
making security offerings in the markets of the United States,
and that such Board and Federal reserve banks will not under-
take to pass upon such offerings.
